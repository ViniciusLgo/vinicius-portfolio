When I started in tech — I believe like most people — it was through support. My job was to keep everything running: answering tickets, fixing printers, swapping parts, researching issues I didn't even understand the root cause of, solving network problems, administering local environments and, over time, cloud environments.

That was day-to-day technical support.

Back then, I had no idea that experience would make such a difference once I started programming.

## What changed when I moved into development

Today, when I'm building something, I don't just think about writing code — I also think about how it will run, be monitored, deployed, and maintained in production.

Having that background taught me that good software isn't just about well-written code. It needs to actually meet requirements **in the environment where it will run**.

## What infra teaches that college doesn't

**Logs are data, not noise.** In infra, you learn to read logs before anything else. In development, that translates into observability and fast diagnosis.

**Services fail.** In production, nothing is guaranteed. That mindset makes you write more resilient code, with real error handling instead of just the happy path.

**Permissions matter.** Security isn't a last-minute detail. Once you've configured Active Directory, firewalls, and access policies, you think about authentication and authorization from the very start of the system.

**Deployment is part of the product.** A system that works locally but doesn't work in production is a system that doesn't work. Period.

## Back to the question

Does your first experience in tech still influence how you work today?

In my case, the answer is yes — very positively. Infra gave me an end-to-end view that few developers have this early in their career.
